I inspect steel rebar that is embedded inside concrete bridge decks. Over time chloride gets in and the rebar develops corrosion pits. Those pits are the early warning for structural risk. We image a concrete core with a 225 kV industrial CT.

Industrial CT is messy. Two things happen at once. First, beam hardening makes the background brightness drift across the image because the concrete filters low energy X rays more along longer paths, so the concrete looks brighter on one side than the other. It is not a flat field, there is a slow gradient. Second, the focal spot and scatter smear every bright feature into a wide fuzzy halo while keeping total signal energy the same. A pit that is truly compact therefore looks like a large glowing cloud, and its apparent volume can be off by a factor of two.

There is also a second trick. The concrete contains round air bubbles from mixing and sometimes powder clumps. Those appear as bright round blobs. The real corrosion pit is elongated along the rebar direction, not round. In some scans a round bubble is actually larger and brighter than the real pit, so just picking the biggest bright blob fails. You have to tell elongated from round by shape, not just by size.

You will write the program that reports the true physical pit volume.

Save it at /app/solve.py. Grader runs python /app/solve.py with a scan path argument and takes the final whitespace token printed on stdout as the volume in cubic millimetres.

We give you one core to develop with at /app/data/scene.rpbv. You may open that file while coding. Hidden grading uses completely different cores that vary in grid size, voxel pitch, pit shape and elongation, bubble sizes and positions, beam hardening gradient strength and direction, background level, scatter width, and noise. They are passed as random temporary paths. Do not hardcode numbers or paths from the example. Hardcoding the example volume fails hidden checks.

The file format is RPBV, a small custom container, all little endian. The first four bytes are ASCII magic RPBV. Bytes four to seven hold uint32 version. Bytes eight to eleven hold uint32 dtype tag where two means signed int16 voxels and sixteen means float32 voxels. Bytes twelve to twenty three hold three uint32 dimensions nx ny nz which are voxel counts along x y z. Bytes twenty four to thirty five hold three float32 scales sx sy sz in millimetres per voxel. These are anisotropic and file dependent so you must read them. Bytes thirty six to thirty nine hold uint32 offset where voxel data starts. From that offset onward there are nx times ny times nz voxels in the declared type, x moves fastest, so linear index for x y z is x plus nx times y plus ny times z.

Each file contains one main corrosion pit that is elongated along the rebar axis and is what we grade. A few files also contain far away round air bubbles or powder specks. Those round artefacts must be excluded. They can be closer than in earlier tasks and can be heavier than the main pit, so size alone is not enough to tell them apart. The pit interior has a flat saturated plateau after inversion, edges are diluted by partial volume and then further smeared by scatter. The concrete matrix brightness drifts slowly across the volume because of beam hardening, plus detector noise.

Why simple counting breaks. A generous brightness level includes the diffuse halo and the gradient makes one side look brighter, so the result can be double the truth or more. A strict level keeps only the brightest core and loses thin elongation that never gets fully bright. No single global level works because brightness, gradient, scatter width and noise all shift per core. What is preserved is total signal under the blur, not how many voxels are over a level. Using that preservation correctly means handling a drifting background, separating elongated from round by shape, and deciding where the faint halo that truly belongs to the pit ends without stepping into a nearby bubble.

We grade at three percent relative error. Straightforward counting is far outside.
