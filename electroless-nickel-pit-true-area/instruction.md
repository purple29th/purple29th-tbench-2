I run plating QC on electroless nickel for automotive connectors. After plating we get tiny pits where hydrogen bubbles stuck to the surface and blocked deposition. Those pits expose base copper and start corrosion.

We inspect panels with a white light interferometer that scans height. The optics lie. The objective lens point spread and vibration of the stage during scanning spread the pit signal outward into a wide fuzzy halo that looks like a shallow dent. On screen a pit that is actually small and deep looks like a big shallow dish. The customer wants the true geometric pit area in square millimeters, not the apparent blurred dish.

You must write the tool that does this.

Save it as /app/solve.py. Grader runs python /app/solve.py with a scan path argument and takes the last whitespace token from stdout as your answer.

We include one panel scan to develop with at /app/data/scene.enpp. You can open that while coding. The hidden evaluation set uses completely different scans that vary in grid shape, pixel pitch, nickel thickness, pit depth, optical blur width, background level, and noise. They are passed as randomly named temporary files. Do not embed numbers or absolute paths from the example file. Any solution that hardcodes the example area fails hidden checks.

File format is .enpp, a small custom container, all little endian. The first four bytes are ASCII magic ENPP. Bytes 4 to 7 hold uint32 version. Bytes 8 to 11 hold uint32 data type tag where 2 means signed int16 and 16 means float32. Bytes 12 to 19 hold two uint32 dimensions nx ny which are pixels along x y. Bytes 20 to 27 hold two float32 scale factors sx sy in millimetres per pixel. These are anisotropic and file dependent so you must read them. Bytes 28 to 31 hold uint32 offset to start of pixel block. From that offset onward there are nx times ny samples in the declared type, x moves fastest, so linear address for x y is x plus nx times y. In the file background nickel is bright, pits are dark, we inverted during export so pits appear bright.

What is inside each file. A single dominant plating pit is the one we grade plus sometimes one or two tiny isolated bright dots far away caused by dust or polishing scratches. Those dots must not be counted. To get rid of them keep only the principal connected structure using 8 neighbour connectivity and choose the component with the most integrated excess signal, not the most pixels. The pit interior has a flat saturated plateau, edges are diluted by partial coverage and then further smeared by optical blur. The rest of the panel is a roughly flat nickel brightness plus sensor noise.

Why naive pixel counting does not work here. If you count everything above a generous brightness you absorb the diffuse halo that surrounds every pit and the result is roughly double the true physical size, sometimes more. If you count only very bright pixels you miss the thin undercut lips that connect to the main pit which never become fully bright because of partial filling and you end up at about half the true size. No single global level works across the line because overall gain, panel thickness, blur width and noise floor all shift per batch. Counting bright pixels is a proxy, integrating total energy is the physical invariant.

Blur conserves counts. That is the key that allows subpixel recovery. But using it correctly is a small pipeline of its own. First get an unbiased estimate of background nickel level without letting the pit itself bias you, next get a robust estimate of background scatter which is noise sigma, then isolate the main pit energy from far specks, then get the real interior signal level that would be seen without blur and without being tricked by noise spikes, then grow outward to capture the faint halo belonging to the pit until the halo average excess falls into noise floor while stopping before you accidentally bridge to a distant speck. Skipping halo capture causes systematic under estimation because a large fraction of energy lives in the halo when blur is wide, including specks causes over estimation.

We grade to three percent relative error. Simple level methods are far outside that window.
