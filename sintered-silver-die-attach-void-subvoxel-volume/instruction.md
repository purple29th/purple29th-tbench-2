I run void inspection on sintered silver die attach for SiC power modules. After pressure sintering trapped solvent and organic burnout gas form sealed outgassing voids between the die and substrate. Those voids raise junction to case thermal resistance and cause hot spots in the field.

We inspect attach layers with a 160 kV nano focus X ray CT. The imaging lies. The tube focal spot is not a point, the scintillator spreads light, and there is scatter in the dense silver and copper. Combined result is a blur that conserves total energy but pushes it outward into a wide fuzzy halo. On screen an outgassing void that is actually compact looks like a large bright cloud. The customer wants the true geometric void volume in cubic millimeters, not the apparent cloud.

You must write the tool that does this.

Save it as /app/solve.py. Grader runs python /app/solve.py with a scan path argument and takes the last whitespace token from stdout as your answer.

We include one die attach scan to develop with at /app/data/scene.ssdv. You can open that while coding. The hidden evaluation set uses completely different scans that vary in grid shape, voxel pitch, silver density, organic residue brightness, scatter width, background level, and noise. They are passed as randomly named temporary files. Do not embed numbers or absolute paths from the example file. Any solution that hardcodes the example volume fails hidden checks.

File format is .ssdv, a small custom container, all little endian. The first four bytes are ASCII magic SSDV. Bytes 4 to 7 hold uint32 version. Bytes 8 to 11 hold uint32 data type tag where 2 means signed int16 and 16 means float32. Bytes 12 to 23 hold three uint32 dimensions nx ny nz which are voxels along x y z. Bytes 24 to 35 hold three float32 scale factors sx sy sz in millimetres per voxel. These are anisotropic and file dependent so you must read them. Bytes 36 to 39 hold uint32 offset to start of voxel block. From that offset onward there are nx ny nz samples in the declared type, x moves fastest, so linear address for x y z is x plus nx times y plus ny times z. In the file dark voids were inverted during export so outgassing voids appear bright.

What is inside each file. A single dominant sealed outgassing void is the one we grade plus sometimes one or two tiny isolated bright dots far away caused by residual organic inclusions or peripheral microporosity. Those dots must not be counted. To get rid of them keep only the principal connected structure using 26 neighbour volumetric connectivity and choose the component with the most integrated excess signal, not the most voxels. The void interior has a flat saturated plateau, edges are diluted by partial volume and then further smeared by the X ray blur. The rest of the attach layer is a roughly flat silver matrix brightness plus detector noise.

Why naive voxel counting does not work here. If you count everything above a generous brightness you absorb the diffuse halo that surrounds every void and the result is roughly double the true physical size, sometimes more. If you count only very bright voxels you miss the thin vent channels and dendritic fingers that connect to the main bubble which never become fully bright because of partial filling and you end up at about half the true size. No single global level works across the product line because overall gain, die thickness, blur width and noise floor all shift per batch. Counting bright voxels is a proxy, integrating total energy is the physical invariant.

Blur conserves counts. That is the key that allows subvoxel recovery. But using it correctly is a small pipeline of its own. First get an unbiased estimate of background silver level without letting the void itself bias you, next get a robust estimate of background scatter which is noise sigma, then isolate the main void energy from far specks, then get the real interior signal level that would be seen without blur and without being tricked by detector noise spikes, then grow outward to capture the faint halo belonging to the void until the halo average excess falls into noise floor while stopping before you accidentally bridge to a distant inclusion. Skipping halo capture causes systematic under estimation because a large fraction of energy lives in the halo when scatter is wide, including specks causes over estimation.

We grade to three percent relative error. Simple level methods are far outside that window.
