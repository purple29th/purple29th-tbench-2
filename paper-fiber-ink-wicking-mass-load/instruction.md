I work in a photo paper mill grading 120gsm rag with calendered cellulose base and kaolin clay coating applied by blade coater. We print a solid dye patch, then measure how far it feathers along fiber axis because that span tracks Cobb sizing and sheet caliper better than gravimetric mg. A UV fluorescence microscope scans it and writes a 2D container we call inkw.

Please make a file at /app/solve.py. We run python /app/solve.py /tmp/input_*.inkw and take the last printed number as span in millimeters.

There is one example at /app/data/scene.inkw for local debugging. Real grading uses entirely new maps I create at runtime with random names like input_a3f9.inkw, new dimensions, pixel pitch, thickness, brightness, blur, background, seed. Hardcoding twelve mm from sample fails hidden.

Container spec little endian. I describe byte ranges not C struct. Bytes zero to three hold ASCII INKW, four to seven version uint32, eight to eleven dtype id two means int16 sixteen means float32, twelve to nineteen two uint32 width height, twenty to twenty seven two float32 sx sy pixel pitch in mm that varies per sheet and must be read, twenty eight to thirty one uint32 payload start that can be thirty two forty forty eight sixty four eighty ninety six one twenty eight so respect it, forty to forty three float32 post coat multiplier that scales mm result and varies zero point eight five to one point three five per file. Sample has one point zero so you do not see effect, hidden cases like offset ninety six use one point two two and fail if ignored. Rest up to start is zero padding. At payload start you have width times height samples in dtype, x fastest index x plus width times y.

What counts. Inside sheet there are ink locations. Real feathered wicking follows fiber and is ragged and stretched along fiber direction, not circular. We also see circular condensation beads from humidifier that are almost round and must be skipped. And we see tiny far window dust specks in corners near five five and seventy five seventy five far from center that must be skipped. Some sheets have two distant feathered blots both along fiber, both count, you must add their recovered areas before span conversion. Keeping only single biggest loses forty to fifty percent in those sheets.

How we decide in lab without giving away code. I estimate paper base glow from dimmest four fifths of histogram, noise from median absolute deviation of lower half around a few counts. Then I mark fluorescence that rises more than five noise units above base as candidate. I group with eight connected flood. For each group I look at bounding box of its candidate mask. We keep groups whose box is clearly fiber stretched: ratio of long edge to short edge above one point six, long edge at least ten pixels and not more than thirty five pixels. That keeps ragged feathered wicking and rejects compact beads whose ratio is at most one point six. We also drop far dust by integrated brightness: keep only groups whose total residual above base is at least thirty five percent of strongest group. That drops specks at five five.

Why simple cutoff fails. Low cutoff includes whole aureole from fiber scattering and lens blur and roughly doubles my mm. High cutoff clips ragged tails that are dim because of partial pixel fill and loses third to half. Board has varying base, multiplier, blur width, so no fixed number works.

What saves us is photons are conserved, only spread. Sum of base subtracted glow over feathered blot plus its faint skirt divided by true core level gives true pixel count. Then area mm2 from sx sy, span mm as area divided by fiber repeat zero point four mm times multiplier. You first undo blur to get area, then convert to span.

If you nail base and core you are within a percent, we grade three percent.
